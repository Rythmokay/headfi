## Reviews & Audio Impressions

### Review #1 by  (Rating: 4.50 star(s))

S.M.S.L R100 - R2R DAC

Finally I got my hands on an R2R DAC.

I was getting so tired of trying to compare the pure silicone of Delta-Sigma ESS and AKM chips, so I really hope I can hear some stand-out differences with these R2R or rather, multibit R-2R ladder chips – specifically the AD1861-J 18-bit monolithic PCM chips.

This is going to be kind of a big deal some for of you.

S.M.S.L found a batch of these vintage chips, which were quite a staple of mid to higher end CD players and CD transports throughout the 1990’s and they have chosen to bring this technology into the modern era.

Unlike the more well known discrete type of R2R ladder DACs, put together with multiple separate components, which specifically mainly refers to the resistors which all in row appear to look like a ladder in the electronic schematic.

The R2R ‘multibit’ chips in the R100 are doing the same thing, but directly in a single piece of silicone on a smaller scale.
Disclaimer

Many thanks to S.M.S.L for providing this R100 unit for the purpose of this review.

They have not asked me to say anything about it, all opinions are my own.
Build, Design & Features

First of all, you can have it in any colour you want, as long as it’s silver.

I actually like the way the industry is mostly moving away from pure, boring black boxes. S.M.S.L has begun adopting a series of concave waves as part of their most recent aesthetic, and I’m all for it.

It’s simple, subtle and classy.

Not only that, but the whole chassis is a full CNC aluminium box, so it’s just beautiful from all angles.

Another great touch are the windows into the DAC from above.

I wish all my components had this.

Just as I love tearing open all my headphones to look at the drivers, it’s great to see the care and attention to the details of the internal design and when a company is proud enough to show off their work.

There is also a small incentive in some cases to spread out the components in this way to help with heat dissipation, but I suspect the biggest incentive here was eye candy.

A funny little extra are the yellowish white LEDs that light up the channel names.

I don’t hate it, but there is no reason for this. I think I would have preferred if these LEDs actually lit up the components instead.

The whole DAC is not a super heavy weight at just over 700 grams, but it feels reassuringly worth it’s asking price, whatever that means, and sits well on the desk, especially after some chunky XLR cables are added.

On the front you only have the display and two buttons which cycles through and indicates the currently selected EQ profile and input.

The feel of pressing the buttons is really fine. They are very low action digital buttons and don’t have any slop or wobble.

On the rear is a delightfully fully packed selection of inputs and outputs.

It’s really got everything you expect at this price – RCA and XLR outputs, USB-C, Optical and Coaxial inputs and the ‘kettle plug’ power socket.

I actually very much appreciate the visually balanced and intuitive design with outputs on the outer edges.

Underneath we’ve got some nice chunky silicone-bottomed feet that give it more poise despite is medium-small size. They’ve also hidden the screws here, maximising the aesthetics from all other angles.

Moving onto the digital features, it’s a simple matter of hooking your R100 up to your PC or laptop and opening your world wide web browser of choice and navigating to 
https://dsptuner.com
 where you are prompted to “Start tuning” and connect your compatible device.

The EQ GUI should then open up and allow you to save two separate EQ profiles.

This is especially useful for those who frequently switch between loudspeakers and headphones, as it’s quite common to set a very unique EQ profile for your loudspeakers as their response will always change depending on the room they are placed in.

Then you’ve got a 2nd EQ profile for your exotic headphone of choice.

What I absolutely love about this, is that while you are limited to saving only two profiles to the DAC, you can locally save as many profiles as you like and just reupload them to the R100 whenever you like, or even, do crazy stuff like sharing profiles online – which I actually do not condone, because your personal settings are likely not to work perfectly with the tastes or more importantly, the HRTF of others.

The UI is extremely intuitive, easy to use, and as far as I can tell, bug free so it’s a really neat, well thought out, and almost perfectly complete feature.

I do have two minor complaints, however, the first is that it is limited to 10 bands.

On the other hand, very few great headphones will need that many bands, but it’s always nice to have more control and never need it, than to have too few.

The other minor complaint is that on the device the profiles are labelled EQ1 and 2, while in the software, they are listed as A and B.

My only major complaint is that there is actually no reset to default feature that sets a flat EQ from where to easily create a new profile, so I recommend making a flat curve before you start and saving that as a custom profile that you can use as a blank canvas in future.

Finally, there is one feature that some may find a bit annoying. Just as music starts or is paused or stopped, the relay in the DAC clicks on or off, completing or breaking the circuit, so that the DAC is completely muted when there is no audio playing. This is ultimately a higher end component than the digital alternative, so the small extra click is the trade-off.
The Sound

Finally, the most important part.

It sounds great.

While we’re still on the topic of EQ profiles, the R100 profiles automatically reduce the pre-amp for any boost you make, so you don’t have to worry about only negative EQ’ing, but it does mean that strong bass shelves, for example, will set the volume quite low, so switching to no-EQ mode will result in a sudden global volume boost, so that’s something to be wary of.

For the nitty-gritty A/B comparison I compared the R100 directly against the Fosi Audio ZH3, mainly fed into the STAX SRM-007ta hooked up the STAX SR-009, and the Burson Audio Soloist Stellar with various headphones.

While the R100 is an 18 bit R2R monolithic resistor-ladder with AD1861-J chips, the ZH3 uses a 32 bit AKM AK4493SEQ delta-sigma chip; incidentally it is also a combo DAC and headphone amp, but boasts excellent specs and like the R100 also has XLR line out.

On paper, the ZH3’s AKM chip sounds like the easy pick with lower THD, native DSD support to the few who need it, and a higher dynamic range.

While A/Bing the two, which was made considerably easier with the SoundSwitch app, I found that it was ultimately a very close race.

AKM chips are already frequently celebrated by the larger audiophool community for apparently providing a smoother, less analytical performance compared to the razor-sharp  sound of ESS chips, for example.

So theoretically, this should be an even closer race against a vintage legend, although it’s worth pointing out that S.M.S.L have implemented 16x oversampling to the AD1861-J chip to achieve 18 bit output, and and de-glitch circuitry to clean up the signal, which overall should theoretically sound more controlled and precise compared to these chips in vintage hardware, but should also not eliminate it’s fundamental character.

A very bad analogy to this would be like dropping in a modern engine with new suspension and modern tyres on a vintage sports car, but in this case, all the modern ‘turbocharging’ is permanent and there is no switch to run this in NOS or non-oversampling mode.

Anyhow, I’ve often thought that the differences in DACs are so small, it’s often not even worth worrying about.

In this instance, I would argue the same is mostly true, about 70% of the time, but this number might fluctuate wildly depending on your music library. So far, I’ve found that in about 30% of the music I personally listen to, the R100 presents music with an incrementally greater sense of instrument depth.

I think this gives instruments a slightly bigger scale, a more accurate sense of position in the head-space and something like a faster transient with a more accurate note decay, and the very occasional extra holographic out-of-the-head soundstage.

Ultimately, I was switching back and forth, sometimes listening to the same 3-5 seconds about 30 times and most of the time not coming to any solid conclusions, and every now and then it genuinely felt like where were some real “ah-ha” moments that surprised me with what I can only describe as an incrementally greater sense of realism. So please take these impressions with a huge fistful of salt.
Final Thoughts

Ultimately I do believe that the R100 is a step up over typical entry level DACs, as it occasionally brought me a small step closer to what my ears describe as sounding ‘real’, but whether it’s worth the extra money is entirely up to you.

In the end, I appreciate S.M.S.L for trying something entirely novel that absolutely competes, at least neck and neck with the surrounding competition, while using chips that are over 35 years old.

Call it what you want; be it a vintage revival novelty, a technical exercise, or even a marketing gimmick, there is no denying that at the end of the day, it mostly sounds very good, and occasionally great.

It ticks every single one of my boxes and gets bonus points for vintage goodness, so it get’s a full pass from me.
