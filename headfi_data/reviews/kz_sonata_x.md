## Reviews & Audio Impressions

### Review #1 by  (Rating: 4.50 star(s))

Introduction

I was contacted by Kate from Knowledge Zenith and was invited to test the new Sonata X 28 BA IEM from KZ. The Sonata X is the company's latest flagship model, and is a development of the original Sonata. Like its predecessor, it is an all-BA design with 14 units per channel and retails for $199.

Product purchase link:
https://www.kztws.com/products/kz-sonata-x

The Packaging

The Sonata X comes in a large rectangular black box with a printed sleeve bearing a large image of the IEM, below which some of the salient features are displayed. The KZ logo appears in the top left and there is branding on the sides as well. Removing the sleeve reveals a plain black box with a textured finish and a simple embossed KZ logo in the centre. The box has a hinged opening and inside, the IEMs are displayed in a cut-out. Below this is a grid holding the eartips. On the left in a plain grey box is the blue leatherette carrying case in which the two cables and the tuning lever are stored.

The contents comprise:

● KZ Sonata X IEMs

● Flat QDC silver plated cable

● Braided 784 core blue/silver cable

● Three pairs black memory foam tips

● Three pairs black "Starline" silicone tips

● Leather zipped case

● Tuning lever

● Documentation

Build and Design

The earpieces are constructed of black resin and have a smooth glossy surface. They are somewhat bulky but they have to accommodate 14 drivers! The front of the shell has a clear section behind which is a decorative teal-coloured panel of triangle shapes. The name "Sonata X" appears on the top edge in a script font beside a narrow horizontal grille. There is a small circular pinhole vent on the underside and the clear plastic 2-pin socket is mounted on the top surface. The four tuning switches are mounted on the rear of the shell and operate as below:

Switch 1 On: Bass increased by 1dB

Switch 1&2 On: Bass increased by 2dB

Switch 3 On: Ultra-high frequency reduced by 1 dB

Switch 3&4 On: Ultra-high frequency reduced by 2 dB

Internally, there are 14 balanced armatures per channel in the following configuration:

1 x 22955 BA* (Low frequency)

2 x 29869 ( Mid-frequency)

1 x 30019 (Mid-high frequency)

5 x 31768**(Ultra-high frequency)

** dual BA array = 10 units

• Impedance: 22-50Ω

• Sensitivity: 110dB

• Frequency Response: 20Hz - 40KHz

*The 22955 bass driver is the new 2nd generation version which employs a venting system.

A 4-way crossover network separates the various frequency bands and the 3D printed structure includes a series of sound tubes to channel the sound to the nozzle.

Two cables are included, the standard KZ silver-plated type with a 90° angled 3.5mm plug and a high quality silver/blue braided 8-core cable which features 48 strands per core (a total of 784 strands). It has a straight 3.5mm plug. I would have preferred a modular version of the high quality cable instead of having the two different cables, which would provide balanced operation (see below).

Fit and Isolation

The Sonata comes with a set of medium foam tips pre-fitted. I have large ear canals and prefer silicone tips but none of the supplied tips provided a good seal. After some experimentation I settled on large Spiral Dots which worked perfectly for me and I obtained a very comfortable fit with excellent isolation. Although bulky, the earpieces remained stable and snug. Having decided on the tips I turned my attention to the cable. I tried the stock blue/silver cable but found the sound lacked body. Suspecting that more power was needed I changed to a 4.4mm balanced cable from Noble Audio. The extra power transformed the sound and this combination was used for the review.

The principal source used for testing was a Fiio M21 DAP. The digital filter was set to NOS and high gain with DRE enabled. A Ruark R1 digital radio was also employed and a burn-in period of 100 hours was carried out before evaluation. I found that a higher volume than normal was needed to bring out the best from the Sonata X.

After trying various combinations of the tuning switches, I settled on 1111. This enhanced the bass and brought it in good balance with the mids whereas turning off switches 3 and 4 emphasised the upper frequencies too much which affected the overall balance.

Sound Impressions

My preference is for a deep sub-bass with good weight, but not at basshead levels and a punchy mid bass which does not colour the midrange. The midrange itself should be clean and transparent with good projection of vocals and solo instruments and the treble should be airy, well extended and highly detailed without harshness. The staging should be expansive but not unnatural in extent and the overall tonality should be as authentic as possible with a realistic and natural timbre.

Tonality

The Sonata X impressed with a well-balanced, lively presentation full of detail and a large soundstage. The new 22955 vented driver produced a solid and nicely textured bass with good speed and excellent resolution. There was a good deal of slam in the mid bass, with a tonality just a touch warmer than neutral. Extension was good whilst the profile was somewhat linear. Sub bass displayed some rumble and there was a natural breathy quality to the lower frequencies. Midrange was very clear and expressive with good detail and clean vocal projection. The treble was perhaps the best feature, being smooth, crisp and very extended with a good deal of micro-detail on offer and no evidence of "BA timbre". Like many other quality all-BA models, it excels in reproducing strings. The staging was expansive in all three dimensions with good imaging and stereo movement.

Bass

As described above, the new driver produced a bass which might be described as a hybrid between a BA and DD with the characteristic speed and precision of a balanced armature and some (but not all) of the weight and power of a dynamic unit. The lowest region was fairly linear and mid bass was lively and dynamic. There was a natural and smooth transition into the midrange with no bleed or colouration.

The superb Andante recording of Roy Harris's Symphony No.6 (Gettysburg) by the Pacific Symphony Orchestra under Keith Clark was handled impressively by the Sonata X. In the second movement, "Conflict", plaintive brass chords, accompanied by timpani, introduce the piece. Soon strings are added augmented by bass drum. The natural and airy timbre of the drums was authentic with the rebound of the skin clearly audible and although the BA driver did not produce the physicality of a DD, the Sonata X delivered plenty of slam here. As the piece develops, the feeling becomes more intense. Syncopated chords join with densely scored percussion culminating in a massive strike on bass drum with the power and impact preserved brilliantly.

David Essex's "Rock On" from 1973 features prominent use of bass guitar. Herbie Flowers's double-tracked instruments continue throughout the track forming a solid foundation and providing harmonic cues as the piece contains no instruments playing chords. The simple yet imaginative production by Jeff Wayne really works, with the depth, texture and impact of the bass guitars and kick drum effectively conveyed. The Sonata X's detailed and highly resolving bass allowed the notes to reach the lowest octaves and growl menacingly while remaining clearly separated from the kick drum playing simultaneously. The syncopated strings and brass with minimalist vocals provided a contrast which was handled very well.

Midrange

The midrange was a standout feature of the Sonata X, being open, clear and expressive. Although somewhat forward in nature, there was still a good impression of depth to the image. Vocals projected well above their accompaniment, solo instruments occupied their intended spaces and timbre was exceptionally clean, clear and natural. In addition, there was a lively and dynamic quality to the presentation which encouraged further listening.

"L'heure exquise" is a beautiful contemplative piece by Venezuelan composer Reynaldo Hahn. Scored for cello with piano accompaniment, Mischa Maisky and Daria Hovora's fine performance is characterised by a concentrated atmosphere, with the silences between the phrasing almost as important as the notes themselves. This came over undiluted on the Sonata X with the timbre of the cello and piano natural and authentic and the interplay between the two instruments skilfully handled while the accurate reproduction of the hall ambience added to the realism.

"Adios" is a romantic ballad by Linda Ronstadt from the album "Cry like a rainstorm, howl like the Wind". Her clear voice and perfect diction came over very effectively on the Sonata X, which emphasised the emotional message of the lyrics. The layout of the various performers was nicely portrayed with Linda Ronstadt's lead vocal perfectly balanced with the backing singers and the spacious nature of the recording venue expertly reproduced.

Treble

The fine extension, intricate detail and smooth, airy nature of the Sonata X's treble was a joy to hear. The tuning of the 10 31736 BAs was very impressive with a clean, crisp tonality free of harshness enabling the listener to not only hear what was being played, but also how it was being played. This endowed the upper frequencies with a particularly musical quality, increasing the enjoyment.

Robert Carty is an American ambient music artist with a large number of albums to his name, chiefly in the Space Music and New Age genres. "Beautiful Attractor" is a track from his album "In Neptune's Wake". Based on a shifting electronic background redolent of ocean waves, a meandering synth melody winds its way over a rich bass foundation with swirling electronic effects and crystalline percussive elements spiralling around it. The sheer wealth of detail provided by the Sonata X was very impressive, making the most of the wide and deep soundstage.

Borodin's famous Nocturne from the String Quartet No.2 received a wonderful performance from the Guildhall String Ensemble and the Sonata X rose to the occasion admirably. The string tonality was spot on with lively dynamics, crisp transients and an airy clean timbre. The details of the bowed and plucked sections were nicely differentiated giving a real sense of a live performance. There is a passage where the cello is playing pizzicato, the viola is tremelando and the two violins are displaying the melody. This was beautifully handled by the Sonata X with the musical aspect balanced by its fine technical ability.

Soundstage

The Sonata X delivered a wide, expansive soundstage with a good impression of height and despite the midrange being a little forward, this did not diminish the effect of depth, although this dimension wasa little reduced compared to the width and height. Imaging was precise with plenty of space around solo instruments and lead vocals and the movement around the stage was very well handled.

Mussorgsky's "Pictures at an Exhibition" orchestrated by Ravel is a staple of the concert hall and Riccardo Muti's recording with the Philadelphia Orchestra is perhaps one of the finest available. The first part after the introductory "Promenade" is "Gnomus" depicting a gnome walking on crooked legs. Beginning with a descending run on the strings, brass chords and percussion strikes follow. The dynamic nature and asymmetric rhythms of the piece were conveyed very effectively by the Sonata X with the location of the various sections of the orchestra easy to discern, giving a vivid panorama of the players and evoking a mental picture of the gnome in the imagination.

Kevin Kendle is a British electronic music artist with an extensive catalogue of albums of minimalist electronic music. Amongst his collection he has produced a series of albums inspired by the seasons. "Melting Snow" is from the album "Spring" and the track opens with the sound of running water and birdsong which came over very realistically on the Sonata X with excellent width and copious detail. A series of rising chords, followed by smooth synth patches ensues with a free form improvised woodwind voice providing melodic interest. Delicate electronic arpeggios and percussive touches are added and all these elements came together allowing the Sonata X to produce a vast spherical soundscape full of detail leaving the listener lost in the music.

Comparisons

KZ Sonata (14BA) ($150)

The Sonata is KZ's previous flagship model and, like the new version, is equipped with 14 balanced armatures per channel, one for bass, two for midrange, one for mid/high frequency and five dual BAs for the high frequencies. The principal difference is that the Sonata X has the new vented 22955 driver replacing the original 22955 design.

The Sonata has an expansive soundstage and a midrange displaying excellent detail and expression. The treble is highly resolving, somewhat brighter than neutral,  while the bass is nicely textured, deep and impactful. There is an overall feeling of refinement and maturity and the timbre throughout is largely natural and musical. The layering and separation is very impressive, with instrumental positioning accurate and imaging clear and precise.

Unsurprisingly the Sonata and Sonata X share many similarities. Their profiles are generally neutral/bright with a somewhat forward midrange, good detail retrieval and a large soundstage. Resolution is superior in the Sonata X which displays a more lively and dynamic presentation and a cleaner and airy quality to the mids and treble. The original Sonata has a slightly sharper quality in the high frequencies which does occasionally sound unnatural but that is absent in the  Sonata X which is cleaner and somewhat smoother. In the midrange, the X is warmer with a little more body which must be attributed to the new bass driver. This warmth emerges from the upper bass but does not colour the midrange adversely,  rather it endows this region with an attractive bloom.

The X's bass has more weight than the original while retaining similar speed, detail and resolution. Extension is equal in both models and is fairly linear as may be expected from a BA driver. Where the two differ is in the decay and in tonality. The X has a longer natural decay while the original cuts off quickly in comparison.  This could be imputed to the vented design in the new 22955 unit. The X's tonality is warmer and inviting with better energy and projection, producing a entertaining and lively effect which I found preferable.

Kiwi Ears Orchestra II (10BA) ($349)

The Orchestra II is an upgrade to the original Orchestra which came out in 2021. There are 10 custom BAs per channel in the following configuration:

● 2 Transparent Low-Frequency BAs

● 4 Mid-Frequency BAs

● 2 High-Frequency BAs

● 2 Ultra-High-Frequency BAs

The Orchestra II has a remarkably clean, well-balanced and detailed sound with a notable lack of colouration. The overall tonality is on the bright side of neutral with the speed and immediacy expected from an all-BA design. Bass is fast, textured and resolving with a decent amount of weight, sub bass displaying some rumble and mid bass nicely contoured with a clean punch. There is no influence from the bass in the lower mids which are clean and detailed while the rest of the midrange is open and very transparent. This quality continues into the treble with a good consistency of tonality producing a smooth transition. Detail retrieval is outstanding and there is a nice sense of air in the highest frequencies, with occasionally a touch of spice coming through. Soundstage is very well done with stereo movement, layering and separation all praiseworthy while imaging is particularly fine with positioning of elements within the stage precisely defined.

Compared to the Sonata X, the Orchestra II sounds more technical, with somewhat higher resolution and more detail. Soundstage is roughly on a par with perhaps a little more depth as the mids are very transparent and not as forward. Bass extension also improves on that of the Sonata X but does not have the same character, being a little sterile whereas the Sonata X is warmer and more forgiving. I would also have preferred a little more "body" in the Orchestra II's presentation. In the treble the Sonata X is more linear whereas that little bit of spice in the Orchestra II's upper range affects the overall balance. Despite the price difference, there is not a lot to choose between the two models, it being a question of preference. In this respect the Sonata X acquitted itself very well indeed.

Ziigaat Luna (6BA) ($379)

The Luna has a total of six drivers per channel from premium makers Sonion and Knowles. There are two BAs each for bass, midrange and treble. The Luna is very transparent to the source so its character will vary according to the partnering equipment, which of course is as it should be. The twin Sonion units create a powerful bass foundation which almost matches that of a high quality DD whilst possessing the speed and detail of a BA. It has smooth and expressive mids and a very extended and detailed treble. There is an expansive soundstage and the overall tonality is smooth and slightly warm with musicality an overriding feature.

Compared to the Sonata X, the Luna is warmer and more natural with a sound quality encouraging long listening sessions. The Sonata X is brighter, more urgent and cleaner with more apparent detail. It scores highly in technicalities but is also musically expressive, but perhaps not so much as the Luna.

The Luna's twin Sonion bass drivers deliver a warmer, deeper low frequency with a more prominent rumble and a longer decay whereas the Sonata X is faster, cleaner and perhaps more detailed while at the same time not being quite as natural sounding. Mid bass is also warmer but does not display such a punchy character as the Sonata X and there is a slight influence on the mids, which is absent in the KZ design.

The Luna has a smooth and natural midrange which is slightly laid back contrasting with the cleaner more forward mids in the Sonata X which is better at projecting vocals and solo players. The Luna is therefore perfect for relaxing whereas the Sonata X is more engaging, drawing you in to the music.

Conclusion

The Sonata X improves on the original with a more powerful bass courtesy of the revised 22955 driver and a well-judged retune of the midrange and treble. Add an expanded soundstage and a perfect blend of the technical and musical, and you have a recipe for success. The original is already a very good IEM but the changes in the new model give it most appropriately, the "X factor". Where the original managed, at best, to keep pace with its rivals, the X pushes ahead in many respects.

In absolute terms, I would have liked a little more power and weight in the bass and a bit of extra depth in the stage. The midrange and treble were pretty spot on, however.

The presentation is very good but the tip selection could have been wider and a modular cable is almost standard at this price, some of the Sonata X's rivals featuring better presentation and accessories, especially in respect to cables and tips.

With the Sonata X, extensive tip rolling and cable swapping are therefore recommended. The Sonata X is quite a power hungry IEM and a powerful DAP or dongle DAC/AMP is required to bring out its best qualities. Balanced operation helps in this regard and I did also find it source sensitive. Once these aspects have been addressed I can confidently say that the Sonata X is KZ's finest model I have heard so far and a very good example of a high quality modern multi-BA IEM.
