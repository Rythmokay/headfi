Gustard Audalytic DR70N R2R Streaming DAC Review​
Naturalness, Texture and Musicality at an Accessible Price​
By Sajid Amit | Amplify Audio Reviews

Rating: 5/5 
Introduction​
There is something fascinating about the resurgence of R2R digital-to-analog converters.

For years, delta-sigma DACs have dominated the digital audio market, offering increasingly impressive measured performance at progressively lower prices. Today, even relatively inexpensive DACs can deliver extraordinary resolution, vanishingly low distortion and exceptional signal-to-noise ratios.

Yet R2R technology continues to attract dedicated followers.

Why?

Because for many listeners, digital audio reproduction is about more than technical measurements. It's about how convincingly a DAC reproduces the tonal character of instruments, the texture of voices and the natural relationships between musical notes.

Enter the 
Gustard Audalytic DR70N
.

This compact component combines discrete R2R conversion, a separate native 1-bit DSD architecture, FPGA-based processing and modern network-streaming functionality.

On paper, it offers an impressive collection of features for an affordable digital source.

But the DR70N's strongest argument isn't its specification sheet.
It's how naturally and convincingly it reproduces music.

I've spent time evaluating the DR70N with a range of headphones, including the Sennheiser HD650, HIFIMAN Arya and HIFIMAN Susvara.

What impressed me was the consistency of its presentation across these very different headphones.

In my system, the DR70N produced a more natural, balanced and musically convincing presentation than several other DACs and streamers I've encountered around this price point.

And that deserves a closer look.

Design and Build Quality​
The DR70N is an understated piece of equipment.

Measuring approximately 220 × 170 × 50 mm and weighing around 1.5 kg, it occupies relatively little space on a desktop.

The chassis is constructed from aluminum and feels reassuringly substantial.

There's a certain functional elegance to the design. Audalytic hasn't attempted to make the DR70N look like an extravagant high-end component.

Instead, the emphasis is on a compact, solid enclosure with practical controls and connectivity.

The front panel incorporates a display and touch-sensitive controls, while the rear provides the connections necessary for integrating the unit into a wide range of audio systems.

These include USB, coaxial, optical and Bluetooth digital inputs, alongside balanced XLR and single-ended RCA analog outputs.

The DR70N also incorporates an Ethernet connection for network streaming.

The balanced XLR outputs are particularly welcome at this price, providing compatibility with dedicated headphone amplifiers and more elaborate desktop or two-channel systems.

The DR70N is not an all-in-one headphone amplifier. It requires suitable downstream amplification.

For listeners who already own a capable amplifier, however, this separation of responsibilities is hardly a disadvantage.

Indeed, part of the appeal of a dedicated DAC is the flexibility to experiment with different amplification and headphone combinations.
What's Inside: Discrete R2R Conversion​
The DR70N's principal attraction is its discrete resistor-ladder conversion architecture.

Most affordable DACs employ delta-sigma conversion, typically through integrated converter chips from manufacturers such as ESS, AKM or Cirrus Logic.

Audalytic takes a different approach.

Its proprietary discrete R2R ladder handles PCM conversion, while DSD signals are processed through a separate native 1-bit architecture.

An FPGA manages digital processing, with a dedicated precision clocking system supporting the overall design.

It's important not to romanticize R2R technology.

A resistor ladder doesn't automatically sound better than a delta-sigma converter. Implementation quality, power supply design, analog output stages and numerous other engineering considerations remain critically important.

Nevertheless, R2R architectures offer a technically distinct approach to digital conversion.

And in the DR70N, that approach appears to have been implemented with considerable attention to musical presentation.

I particularly appreciate that Audalytic hasn't limited the product to a narrowly defined set of digital sources.

Instead, the DR70N combines its conversion architecture with modern connectivity and network-audio functionality.

That makes it considerably more versatile than a traditional standalone R2R DAC.
Network Streaming: More Than Just a DAC​
The DR70N is fundamentally different from the standard DR70 in one important respect.

It incorporates dedicated network-streaming hardware.

Its Ethernet interface allows it to operate as a network-audio endpoint, with support for protocols including Roon Bridge, AirPlay, UPnP/DLNA and HQPlayer NAA.

For listeners using compatible software and network-based music libraries, this can eliminate the need for a separate external streamer.

The DAC and network front end are integrated into a single compact component.

That has obvious advantages for desktop systems, where available space is often limited.

It also makes the DR70N a logical digital source for a conventional two-channel stereo system.

The networking functionality is an important part of the product's versatility, although the sonic impressions in this review should not be interpreted as an exhaustive assessment of every supported streaming protocol.

What interests me most is the combination of modern streaming connectivity with a discrete R2R conversion architecture.

That combination is becoming increasingly accessible, and the DR70N is an attractive example.

Sound Quality​
Overall Presentation: Naturalness Above All​
The defining characteristic of the DR70N is its naturalness.

That's a word frequently used in audiophile reviews, sometimes without much explanation.

In this case, I mean something specific.

The DR70N has a convincing sense of tonal balance and continuity. Instruments possess appropriate body, vocals have substance, and individual notes seem to form part of a coherent musical performance rather than appearing as isolated sonic events.

There's nothing artificially spectacular about the presentation.

The DR70N doesn't attempt to impress through exaggerated treble brilliance, hyper-etched outlines or an unnaturally illuminated soundstage.

Instead, it prioritizes tonal credibility.
And in my listening, that quality makes it more enjoyable than several competing DACs and streamers around its price.

What particularly surprised me was how consistently this characteristic emerged across different headphones.

That brings me to one of the most interesting aspects of my evaluation.
Listening With the HD650, Arya and Susvara​
I've used the DR70N with headphones ranging from the Sennheiser HD650 to the HIFIMAN Arya and the considerably more demanding Susvara.

These headphones differ substantially in their presentation and technical capabilities.

The HD650 is known for its intimate, midrange-oriented sound.

The Arya offers a distinctly different presentation, with its planar-magnetic architecture and more expansive spatial character.

The Susvara belongs to an entirely different performance category, capable of revealing subtle differences in associated equipment when paired with suitable amplification.

What impressed me was that the DR70N's strengths remained apparent across all three.
Regardless of which headphone I used, the DR70N consistently delivered a natural, balanced and musically satisfying presentation.

I don't mean that it made the headphones sound identical.

Quite the opposite.

A good source component should preserve the distinctive character of the headphones connected downstream.

What I appreciated was that the DR70N's tonal coherence remained convincing as I moved between headphones with very different sonic signatures and resolving capabilities.

With less expensive source components, I sometimes encounter an emphasis on explicit detail that can make the presentation feel somewhat artificial.

The DR70N generally avoids that tendency.

It allows the music to flow without drawing unnecessary attention to the conversion process.

And importantly, its natural presentation remains appealing even when evaluated through highly resolving headphones such as the Susvara.

I wouldn't suggest that the DR70N suddenly becomes equivalent to a multi-thousand-dollar flagship DAC.

That would be an unreasonable conclusion.

But I do believe it performs unusually convincingly for its price.

Its ability to maintain musical naturalness across such different headphones is one of the strongest reasons I find it appealing.
Bass: Substance Without Exaggeration​
The DR70N produces bass with convincing weight and tonal definition.

This isn't an artificially bass-enhanced presentation.

Rather, low-frequency instruments possess a satisfying sense of body and physical presence.

Acoustic bass has substance, while kick drums retain a convincing relationship between their initial attack and subsequent decay.

For me, good bass reproduction is about more than quantity.

A bass note should have texture, pitch definition and a believable harmonic structure.

The DR70N does well in these respects.

It doesn't appear to sacrifice tonal precision in pursuit of warmth.

That balance is important.

Some components achieve an initially pleasing sense of fullness by emphasizing lower frequencies, but the effect can become tiring or obscure instrumental distinctions over time.

The DR70N avoids sounding excessively thick.

Its bass presentation contributes to the overall sense of naturalness without overwhelming the remainder of the frequency spectrum.
Midrange: The DR70N's Greatest Strength​
If I had to identify one area where the DR70N makes its strongest argument, it would be the midrange.

Vocals possess convincing texture and tonal density.

Piano notes have harmonic substance.

Acoustic instruments sound appropriately full without becoming unnaturally warm.

These qualities contribute significantly to my enjoyment of the DR70N.

There's a pleasing continuity to the presentation.

Notes don't sound as though they've been artificially separated and spotlighted.

Instead, instruments and voices retain their relationship to the broader musical performance.

This becomes particularly attractive with vocal recordings, jazz and acoustic music.

One of the characteristics I value in audio equipment is the ability to reproduce instrumental tone without constantly reminding me that I'm listening to a digital system.

The DR70N performs well in this regard.

Its presentation is smooth without sounding excessively softened, and detailed without being aggressively analytical.

This balance is central to its appeal.
Treble: Detailed Without Being Etched​
The DR70N's treble follows the same philosophy as its midrange.

High-frequency information is present, but it isn't pushed forward to create an exaggerated sense of resolution.

Cymbals and upper harmonics remain integrated within the overall tonal balance.

The result is a presentation that I find easy to enjoy for extended periods.

This is particularly important with headphones capable of revealing considerable treble detail.

An overly aggressive digital source can make an otherwise resolving system sound unnecessarily sharp.

The DR70N avoids that impression in my listening.

It doesn't rely on artificial brightness to communicate detail.

Instead, it allows high-frequency information to emerge naturally within the recording.

Listeners who prefer an especially brilliant, explicitly analytical presentation may favor a different DAC.

For my preferences, however, the DR70N's balance between resolution and restraint works very well.
Soundstage and Imaging​
The DR70N also offers a convincing sense of spatial coherence.

Individual instruments occupy stable positions, and the presentation maintains a reasonable sense of depth and separation.

What I particularly appreciate is the relationship between the various musical elements.

Some digital components can sound impressive by emphasizing separation to such an extent that instruments appear almost detached from one another.

That can create an initially striking presentation, but it doesn't always correspond to my idea of musical realism.

The DR70N favors a more integrated presentation.

Instruments retain their individual identities without losing the impression that they're performing within a shared acoustic environment.

This is particularly effective with recordings where the interaction between instruments is central to the performance.

The DR70N doesn't manufacture an enormous soundstage.

Rather, it maintains a coherent spatial presentation that supports its overall tonal character.
How Does It Compare With Other DACs at This Price?​
This is ultimately where the DR70N makes its strongest case.

The affordable DAC market has become extraordinarily competitive.

There are numerous devices offering excellent measurements, extensive connectivity and sophisticated conversion architectures at relatively modest prices.

Many are technically impressive.

Some are also exceptionally good listening devices.

The DR70N distinguishes itself through its particular combination of tonal naturalness, instrumental texture and overall balance.
Compared with other DACs and streamers I've heard at comparable prices, I consistently found the DR70N more natural and musically convincing in my system.

This is a subjective assessment rather than a claim of universal technical superiority.

I'm not suggesting that it has lower distortion, greater measured resolution or objectively superior conversion accuracy than every competing product.

Those are separate questions that require controlled measurements.

What I am saying is that its presentation appeals to my listening preferences in a way that several similarly priced alternatives do not.

And the fact that this impression persisted across the HD650, Arya and Susvara gives me greater confidence that I'm responding to a consistent characteristic of the DR70N rather than a particularly fortunate pairing with one headphone.

For listeners who share my preference for natural tone, convincing instrumental body and long-term listening comfort, that's a meaningful advantage.
System Matching and Practical Considerations​
The DR70N is particularly well suited to listeners who already own a capable headphone amplifier.

Its balanced and single-ended analog outputs make it easy to integrate into a desktop system.

The inclusion of network streaming also broadens its appeal to listeners building a conventional stereo system around an integrated amplifier, preamplifier or active speakers.

I would be careful, however, not to confuse versatility with complete functionality.

The DR70N doesn't incorporate a headphone amplifier.

Its user interface is also relatively straightforward compared with more expensive network-streaming components.

While the unit offers volume adjustment, users planning to connect it directly to a power amplifier should verify its volume-control behavior and suitable operating levels before doing so.

These considerations don't undermine the DR70N's value proposition.

They simply reflect its position as a relatively affordable DAC and network-streaming component rather than an elaborate all-in-one audio system.

For listeners who already have suitable amplification, the DR70N offers considerable flexibility.
Final Verdict: An Exceptionally Natural-Sounding Affordable R2R DAC​
The Gustard Audalytic DR70N makes a compelling case for the continued relevance of discrete R2R conversion.

Its combination of a resistor-ladder DAC, separate native DSD architecture, FPGA processing, balanced analog outputs and integrated network streaming is impressive at this price level.

But specifications aren't the main reason I recommend it.

The DR70N's appeal is its presentation.

It delivers music with natural tonal balance, convincing instrumental texture and an unusual degree of coherence.

And importantly, these qualities remained apparent throughout my listening with headphones ranging from the Sennheiser HD650 to the HIFIMAN Arya and Susvara.

That consistency impressed me.

I have encountered numerous affordable DACs that offer excellent technical capabilities.

Not all of them deliver a presentation I find equally satisfying.
The DR70N is one of those relatively inexpensive components that reminds me why musical naturalness matters more to me than specifications alone.

It isn't a giant killer in every conceivable respect.

It doesn't offer the elaborate interface of a premium streamer, nor should anyone expect it to outperform every expensive DAC on the market.

But it gets several important things right.

Its midrange is convincing, its bass possesses appropriate substance, its treble is controlled, and its overall presentation encourages extended listening.

For listeners interested in experiencing discrete R2R conversion without investing heavily in high-end digital equipment, the DR70N deserves serious consideration.

And for enthusiasts building a desktop headphone system or compact two-channel setup, its combination of network connectivity and balanced analog outputs makes it an unusually versatile proposition.
At its price, I consider the DR70N an excellent value and one of the more musically satisfying affordable DACs I've encountered.
